# Task ideas for a one-hour session

Participants often say "I can't think of a task that takes an hour". Three answers before the menu:

1. **It does not have to be one task.** Pick two or three real items from this week's to-do list and work through them in any order. Finishing one and starting the next is exactly the kind of record we want. If everything is done at 40 minutes, the session ends early; that is fine.
2. **It does not have to be finished.** "Make progress on" is enough: plan half the trip, write the first page, narrow ten options to three.
3. **Ask the day before.** The researcher asks "what are three things you need to do on your computer this week?", picks the one that fits the assigned family best, and the participant starts the session already knowing what to do.

Rules that still apply: real to you, on your own computer, nothing that needs passwords or card numbers (stop before paying), no private chats you do not want recorded, no confidential work.

## A. Clear and routine (you know the steps and what "done" looks like)

- Submit an expense report or reimbursement from receipts you have.
- Register for a conference, workshop, or event (stop before payment).
- Put your CV, a paper, or a report into a new template; fix the reference list or bib file.
- Clean up a spreadsheet, merge two data files, rename and sort a folder of files or photos.
- Book the train, bus, or flight you have already decided on (stop before paying).
- Update your website, Google Scholar, LinkedIn, or lab page with things that already happened.
- Set up the calendar invites for a known series of meetings; fill in a timesheet or a form you have the details for.
- Work through routine emails and archive or reply to the non-private ones.

## B. Choose among options (a real choice; criteria still fuzzy)

- A winter coat, boots, headphones, a monitor, a desk chair, a bike, a mattress, coffee gear, within a budget.
- A laptop or phone upgrade; which model and configuration.
- A gym, yoga or climbing studio, a language course, or an online course for a skill you want.
- Courses for next semester: compare syllabi, times, and reviews.
- Which conference or workshop to submit to or attend, given deadlines and money.
- A reference manager, note-taking app, or to-do app to switch to, and how to migrate.
- A restaurant or venue for a specific dinner with specific people.
- A paper for next week's reading group, out of several candidates.

## C. Plan something open (even the shape of the plan is up to you)

- Winter break: a trip, a week at home, or a staycation with things to do in the city.
- Thanksgiving or a visit from family or friends: where, what, who.
- A birthday dinner, a lab social, a group retreat, a workshop you host.
- A holiday gift for someone, starting from only a vague idea.
- Getting into a hobby: a first 10k, climbing, photography, a cuisine, an instrument; what to buy, where to start, a first month.
- Your apartment situation at lease end: stay, move, where, what to look for.
- A conference trip in December: flights, hotel, sessions, people to meet.
- A rough budget or savings plan for next year (no account logins).

## D. Explore and shape an idea (reading and searching until a question takes shape)

- Start from a topic and arrive at a research question, by reading papers and posts.
- Scope a side project: what exists, what you would build, what tools.
- Decide which method, library, model, or statistical test to use for a problem you have now.
- Find the five most relevant recent papers for one sub-problem of your thesis and write a paragraph about them.
- Prepare the outline of a talk, lecture, or tutorial from sources you still have to find.
- Understand why a result looks off: data, documentation, forum threads.
- Learn a tool you need (a library, LaTeX package, Obsidian, a stats package) from its docs by trying things.

## E. Make something from sources (a draft that needs looking things up)

- A fellowship, grant, or application statement that needs facts and references.
- A related-work paragraph or a background section with citations.
- Three to five slides for a lab meeting from material you have to gather.
- Meeting notes or a reading-group summary for the group from several documents.
- A blog post, tutorial, syllabus draft, or project README.
- An email asking for something that needs details: a letter request, a visa letter, a collaboration proposal.
- A comparison table of tools, datasets, or options from web sources.

## For the researcher: matching a to-do item to a family

| If the participant says... | Family |
|---|---|
| "I just need to do X, it's boring" | A |
| "I need to pick one of these" | B |
| "I need to figure out what to do about X" | C |
| "I want to understand / find / decide an approach" | D |
| "I owe someone a draft" | E |

Mixed items are fine: record the family that fits best, and code what actually happened afterwards.
